Measuring the Room in Sixty Seconds

  1. Measure the room's length and width along the floor, then the wall height, usually eight feet.
  2. Count the doors and windows; standard sizes are already deducted at 21 and 12 square feet respectively.
  3. Set the coat count. Two is the honest default for almost every repaint.
  4. Leave coverage at 350 unless your cans state otherwise.
  5. Add your local price per gallon for a budget figure, then press Estimate Gallons.

The Coverage Formula

Wall area is the room's perimeter multiplied by height, minus the openings that swallow no paint:

walls = 2 × (length + width) × height
net = walls − (doors × 21) − (windows × 12)
gallons = ceiling( net × coats ÷ coverage )

Example: a 14 by 12 bedroom with eight-foot ceilings has a perimeter of 52 feet, giving 416 square feet of wall. One door and two windows remove 45 square feet, leaving 371. Two coats demand painting 742 square feet, which at 350 per gallon works out to 2.12, rounding up to three gallons. At $38 each, the paint budget reads $114 before primer, tape, and rollers.

Rounding up matters more here than in most math. Paint varies between batches, so returning for a fourth of a gallon later risks a visible shade difference across the same wall. Leftover paint, meanwhile, stores for years as touch-up supply.

What Changes Real-World Coverage

  • Texture. Orange-peel and knockdown textures hide shadows but drink paint; expect 20 to 30 percent worse spread than smooth drywall.
  • Color jumps. Going from navy to cream needs a gray-tinted primer coat first, effectively adding a third pass.
  • New drywall. Bare board drinks primer like a sponge; always prime new construction before counting topcoats.
  • Application method. Sprayers lose material to mist and need 10 percent more than rolling; cheap roller covers spit and streak, wasting passes.
  • Humidity. Slow-drying conditions extend working time but do not change spread rate much; extreme heat does, by drying edges mid-stroke.

Floors Follow Different Rules Entirely

Wall projects live in area units, but many room makeovers continue onto the floor, and flooring materials refuse to be measured in gallons. Hardwood planks and laminate are sold by the linear foot at a given board width, while boxes quote square footage per carton. When the project crosses from walls to floors, translating between those systems becomes necessary, and a quick way to handle square feet to linear feet for flooring orders prevents both an unfinished strip along one wall and a pallet of surplus planks.

Store-Day Checklist

  • Bring your gallon figure and ask about batch numbers; one batch per room guarantees uniform color.
  • Buy painter's tape, angled brushes for corners, and a quality roller cover in the same trip.
  • If the store mixes custom colors, request a sample pint first; $5 beats repainting a whole wall.
  • Keep the receipt and leftover cans; most retailers accept returns on unopened gallons.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many square feet does one gallon of paint actually cover?

Manufacturers typically quote 350 to 400 square feet per gallon on smooth primed walls. Rough texture, bare drywall, and dramatic color changes can drop real-world coverage toward 250 or 300 square feet, which is why planning with 350 keeps you safe.

Do I really need two coats?

Almost always yes. One coat rarely hides the old color evenly, especially over darker shades or repairs. Two coats deliver uniform color and durability; deep reds and blues over light walls often need a tinted gray primer plus two topcoats.

Should ceilings be included in the wall calculation?

Ceilings are usually painted a different finish and color, so this calculator counts walls only. To add a ceiling, multiply its length by width and divide by your coverage rate as a separate line item.

Is it better to buy one big can or several small ones?

One larger container costs less per gallon and, more importantly, lets you box all the paint together so every wall matches perfectly. Small cans vary slightly between batches, which shows as subtle shading where one can ended and the next began.
